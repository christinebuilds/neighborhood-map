                  What the colors mean
                  • City flood zone (purple):
                  • Past storms (dashed): places news reports say flooded before. Red is a repeat hotspot, orange was reported once or twice. An estimate, not a record of this week.
                  • 311 reports (solid dots): blue is flooding, teal a blocked or broken storm drain, brown a sewage backup. Only open cases from the last day show; closed ones are left off. "311: last" in Layers reaches further back. When there are too many dots to read, the map shows one number for each neighborhood instead; tap it or zoom in to see each report.
                  • Storm drains: pink means check now (a neighbor flagged it, or an open 311 case is within 60 m). Amber means check first (in the flood zone or a past-flood area). Green was cleared by a neighbor in the last 14 days. This is the map's own rule of thumb, not a city assessment. Tap a drain to mark it cleared or flag it. To adopt one officially: the city's adopt-a-drain program.
                  • Neighbor reports (cyan): "water here" posts from the last 24 hours, fainter once they are more than 12 hours old. A brown edge means the neighbor said it looks or smells like sewage. Unverified: this map cannot test the water.
                  • Plan a route (dark line): a driving or walking route that goes around places that flooded in past storms, water neighbors reported in the last 24 hours, and open 311 sewage backup reports. It also tries to stay out of the city flood zone when that adds little time. Solid line: this route. Dotted line: the most direct way. A small label on each line says what it is: a car, a walker, a bus or a train with the line's name, or "most direct". This map can't see water on the road right now. Never drive through water. Turn around.
                  • Weather alert areas (navy, off at first): where a National Weather Service alert for San Francisco applies. Turn it on in Layers, or tap Show on map in Forecast.
                  • Outlook line and Forecast: the hour-by-hour rain (Open-Meteo forecast), National Weather Service alerts and the high tides (NOAA). Heavy rain near high tide drains slowest.
                  • Dashed lines follow the city's own street lines for the streets named in news reports. Circles are a rough area around a corner. How far the water spread along each street is an estimate. No shading does not mean a street is safe.
                  • Plan a route, Transit (blue line): one Muni or BART ride with no transfer (a bus, Muni Metro, a streetcar, a cable car or BART) between your start and end: the suggested ride (solid blue) and the walk to and from the stop or station (dotted blue). It picks the ride that passes the fewest places that flooded in past storms, spots where neighbors reported water recently and open 311 sewage backup reports, then the one with the least walking. Parts of a ride that run underground aren't checked, and this map only knows flooding in San Francisco. There are no times here: "Times and transfers in Google Maps" opens them.
